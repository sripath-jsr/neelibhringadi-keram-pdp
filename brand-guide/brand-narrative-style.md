# Brand Narrative Style

How Kerala Ayurveda tells its story on product pages. This is not about individual word choices (see `brand-tone.md`) — it is about the structures, sequences, and storytelling devices that shape how information flows and how persuasion works.

---

## 1. The Master Narrative

Every Kerala Ayurveda product page tells the same underlying story in the same sequence:

**This formula has worked for decades. Here is what it does. Here is how it feels. Here is when you will see results. Here is what is in it. Here is how it compares. Here is what real people say. Here is how to start.**

Compressed: **Trust → Benefit → Sensation → Timeline → Composition → Differentiation → Proof → Action.**

This is not a funnel. It is a confidence-building arc. The customer should feel more certain at every scroll depth, never less.

---

## 2. The Persuasion Sequence

The PDP follows a deliberate information architecture. Each zone has a job.

### Zone 1: Anchor (Above the Fold)

**Job:** Establish what this is, why it is credible, and what it costs — in that order.

| Element | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Category badge ("HAIR FALL · HAIR GROWTH") | Immediate relevance signal |
| Product name (H1) | Identity |
| Benefit tagline | The one-sentence promise |
| Heritage line | Quiet authority |
| Credential meta | Manufacturing trust |
| Usage headline | Removes "is this hard?" objection before price |
| Price + rating + delivery | Transaction readiness |
| Micro-testimonial | Social proof at the decision point |
| Bundle add-on | Upsell framed as "system" |

**Narrative rule:** The customer should be able to decide to buy without scrolling. Everything below the fold deepens conviction but is not required for action.

### Zone 2: Mechanism (First Scroll)

**Job:** Explain how and why the product works. Shift from "what it does" to "what you will experience."

| Element | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Credentials bar | Heritage data (1945, GMP, ISO, FDA, AYUSH) |
| "Two wins. One formula." | Mechanism in one line |
| Reaction callout | Sensory proof — what happens on first use |
| Sensory testimonial | Validates the sensation with a real person |
| Timeline ("The honest sequence") | Sets expectations honestly |
| Summary stats | Quantified social proof (86 reviews, 4.64★, 93% recommend) |

**Narrative rule:** This zone transitions from rational ("here are the credentials") to experiential ("here is what it feels like"). The sensory callout is the bridge — it gives the customer something to imagine.

### Zone 3: Depth (Deep Scroll)

**Job:** Provide the evidence for customers who need more before committing.

| Element | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Testimonials (dark green section) | Extended social proof with specifics |
| Persona tiles | Self-identification ("Is this for you?") |
| Ingredients (consolidated section) | What is in it, with named functions |
| Triple-milk base | The differentiating process detail |
| What's in / What isn't | Clean-label reassurance |
| Application guide | Remove usage friction |
| Safety section | Responsible disclosure |
| Comparison table | Competitive differentiation |

**Narrative rule:** This zone is reference material, not storytelling. The customer is here because they want detail, not persuasion. Be thorough and structured. Use cards, grids, and tables — not flowing prose.

### Zone 4: Close (End of Page)

**Job:** Leave a lasting impression and provide a final purchase opportunity.

| Element | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Accordion ("Go deeper") | Exhaustive detail for the most cautious buyer |
| Customer reviews | Peer validation with names and verified badges |
| Closing CTA | Heritage echo + action button + per-use price |

**Narrative rule:** The closing mirrors the opening. The heritage statement returns. The price returns. The page ends where it began — with quiet confidence.

## 3. Storytelling Devices

### The Heritage Echo

The same heritage message appears three times on the page, each in a different form:

1. **Heritage line** (above the fold): "Some formulas don't need improving." — understated, italic, secondary position
2. **Credentials bar** (first scroll): "1945 EST." + certifications — data format
3. **Closing CTA** (end of page): "Some formulas don't need improving. This is one of them." — the full statement, with the decisive second sentence

This is not repetition — it is reinforcement through variation. The message gains weight each time it appears because the customer has learned more between each instance.

**Rule:** Key brand messages should appear at least twice on a page — once early (as a claim) and once late (as a conclusion the customer can now verify).

### The Honest Sequence

Rather than promising results and hoping the customer does not ask "when?", the brand leads with a realistic timeline:

> Week 2-3: Less in the shower (fall reduction)
> Month 2+: Visible change — continued improvement

This device works because:
- It sets expectations the product can actually meet
- It frames "no instant results" as a feature ("Root strength comes before visible density")
- It uses the customer's own observation language ("less hair in the shower drain")
- It implies that the brand has enough data to know the sequence

**Rule:** When a product requires patience, build the timeline into the narrative rather than hiding it. Honest timelines are a persuasion tool, not a liability.

### The Sensory Bridge

Between the rational mechanism explanation and the social proof section, the PDP inserts a sensory moment:

> "A cooling sensation on the scalp within minutes. The Neeli is working. Oil absorbs without heaviness."

This is followed immediately by a testimonial confirming the same sensation:

> "The cooling on day one is real — I wasn't expecting it. No greasiness at all."

The brand makes a sensory claim, then a real person validates it — within the same scroll distance. This pairs the brand's authority with peer confirmation.

**Rule:** Sensory claims should always be validated by a testimonial within close proximity. The brand describes what happens; a customer confirms it happened.

### The Self-Selection Frame

Instead of targeting a narrow audience, the persona section asks "Is this for you?" and presents four distinct use cases. The customer picks their own box.

This is more effective than a single target statement because:
- It broadens the addressable audience without diluting the message
- Each persona gets a specific benefit ("Gentle enough for daily use" vs. "Strengthens from the root")
- The question format ("Is this for you?") invites rather than dictates
- The customer who sees themselves in a tile feels the product was made for their specific situation

**Rule:** When a product serves multiple needs, present personas as tiles, not paragraphs. Let the customer self-select. The section header should be a question.

### The Consolidation Pattern

V2 consolidates ingredients, triple-milk, and clean-label info into a single "What's Inside" section rather than three separate sections. This device:

- Reduces scroll fatigue
- Creates a single "ingredients story" with a clear hierarchy (hero herbs → milk base → clean-label reassurance)
- Uses a single section header that frames the entire composition: "Four herbs. Three milks. One formula."

**Rule:** When multiple content types serve the same narrative purpose (answering "what's in this?"), consolidate them under one section header with internal structure, rather than splitting them into separate sections.

### The Comparison as Fact Table

The comparison section never argues. It places three options side by side and lets the data speak:

| Dimension | Neelibhringadi | Generic Oil | Chemical Treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Processing | 11-day classical extraction | Machine-mixed, same day | Pharmaceutical synthesis |
| Side effects | None reported | Pore-clogging, buildup | Irritation, dependency, hormonal |

No adjectives. No "we're better because." Just facts in columns. The customer draws their own conclusion.

**Rule:** Competitive comparisons should never editorialize. State the facts. The product wins when the comparison is honest — if it does not, the comparison should not exist.

## 4. Section Header Patterns

Every content section follows the same header structure:

```
[SECTION LABEL]     ← uppercase, small, letter-spaced, secondary color
[HEADLINE]          ← serif italic, primary color, 1-7 words
[SUBLINE]           ← (optional) one sentence of context
```

### Examples from PDP

| Section Label | Headline | Subline |
|---|---|---|
| COMPLETE HAIR RECOVERY | Two wins. One formula. | — |
| FIRST USE | What you'll feel first | — |
| WHAT TO EXPECT | The honest sequence. | Root strength comes before visible density. |
| IS THIS FOR YOU? | Who uses Neelibhringadi Keram | — |
| WHAT'S INSIDE | Four herbs. Three milks. One formula. | — |
| HOW TO USE | The application guide. | — |
| COMPARE | How it compares | — |
| INFORMATION | Go deeper. | — |
| FEEDBACK | Customer Reviews | — |

### Pattern Rules

1. **Section labels** are functional category names. They tell the customer what kind of content follows. They are never clever.
2. **Headlines** are the editorial voice. They are short, often fragments, and use serif italic. They make the customer curious.
3. **Sublines** appear only when the headline is abstract and needs grounding. "The honest sequence." needs "Root strength comes before visible density." to make sense. "How it compares" does not need a subline.
4. **Headlines use periods.** Even fragments. "Go deeper." not "Go deeper" — the period adds finality and confidence.

## 5. Information Density by Zone

The page deliberately varies how much information it packs per screen.

| Zone | Density | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Above the fold | **High** — name, tagline, heritage, credentials, usage, price, rating, testimonial, bundle, CTA all visible | The decision-ready customer gets everything without scrolling |
| Mechanism (first scroll) | **Medium** — one idea per section, generous padding, centered text | Slow down. Let the mechanism sink in. |
| Depth (deep scroll) | **High** — ingredient cards, grids, comparison tables, step-by-step guides | The research customer wants density. Give it to them in structured formats. |
| Close | **Low** — one statement, one button, one price | End clean. No new information. |

**Rule:** Density should match intent. Decision-ready zones are dense. Persuasion zones are spacious. Reference zones are dense again. The close is sparse.

## 6. Proof Layering

The PDP uses four types of proof, each deployed at the right moment:

### Institutional Proof

Certifications, heritage data, manufacturing standards. Appears early and in data formats.
- "Made by BAMS doctors in our GMP & ISO-certified facility."
- Credentials bar: 1945, GMP, ISO, FDA, AYUSH

### Sensory Proof

What the customer will physically experience. Appears after mechanism explanation.
- "A cooling sensation on the scalp within minutes."
- "Oil absorbs without heaviness."

### Social Proof

What other customers report. Appears at multiple points, escalating in detail.
- Micro-testimonial (pricing zone): one sentence + name + city
- Sensory testimonial (after mechanism): one sentence confirming sensation
- Full testimonials (dark section): multi-sentence stories with timeframes and badges
- Customer reviews (near close): verified purchases with star ratings

### Comparative Proof

How the product stacks up against alternatives. Appears deep in the page.
- Side-by-side table: Neelibhringadi vs. Generic vs. Chemical
- Binary mobile version: Us vs. Them rows

**Rule:** Each proof type has a natural position in the scroll. Institutional proof anchors the top. Sensory proof bridges to social proof. Social proof builds through the middle. Comparative proof closes the argument. Do not put comparative proof above the fold or institutional proof at the bottom.

## 7. The Ritual Frame

The brand does not sell a product — it sells a ritual. This reframing appears consistently:

| Product Language (Avoided) | Ritual Language (Used) |
|---|---|
| Apply the oil | Warm, massage, leave, rinse |
| Use 3 times a week | "Thirty minutes, three times a week" |
| Directions for use | "The application guide" |
| Treatment plan | "The honest sequence" |
| Buy again | Reorder (implies established habit) |
| Product benefits | "Two wins. One formula" |
| Usage instructions | Step 1: WARM / Step 2: APPLY / Step 3: LEAVE |

The steps are given single-word uppercase labels (WARM, APPLY, LEAVE) that read like a ritual prescription, not a set of instructions.

**Rule:** Wherever possible, frame product usage as a practice the customer is adopting, not a task they are performing. The language should make the routine feel intentional, not burdensome.

---

## 8. Closure Pattern

The page ends with the Peak-End Rule applied — the last impression is designed to be the most emotionally resonant.

The closing CTA section contains exactly three elements:

1. **Heritage echo:** "Some formulas don't need improving. This is one of them." — the page's most confident statement, saved for last
2. **Action:** ADD TO CART — ₹315 — the button includes the price, removing one last friction point
3. **Rational anchor:** "~₹10.50 per use · ~30 uses per bottle" — the math that makes the price feel small

No new information. No new benefits. No testimonials. Just the core message, the action, and the math.

**Rule:** The closing section should contain nothing the customer has not already seen. It is a summary, not an argument. One emotional statement. One button. One number.
